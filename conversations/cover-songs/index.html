Why did artists stop releasing cover songs?

Music businessNo. 10US-basedBy Stuart SingletonUpdated November 17, 20267 min read

Stuart asks

Artists used to release covers all the time, and now you barely see them. What happened, and what does an independent artist actually get from recording someone else's song?

The short answer

Covers used to be a normal part of a record: 6 of the 14 songs on the Beatles' first album were covers. According to music writer Chris Dalla Riva, no cover has topped the Billboard Hot 100 since 2001. Luke Combs' version of "Fast Car" came close, reaching No. 2 in 2023. There's no single documented cause, but the money explains a lot: on a cover, the original songwriters get all of the publishing, and many artists now write their own songs and want that share. What a cover still gives an independent artist is a master they own, of a song people already know, under a license nobody can refuse once the song has been released. And every stream pays the person who wrote it.

This is how cover licensing works in the US. Other countries have their own rules.

How common were covers, and how rare are they now?

In the 1960s, covers filled albums. Dalla Riva counts 11 of 13 songs on Bob Dylan's first album and 6 of 14 on the Beatles' first as covers. In his 2023 analysis of every No. 1 hit, he found no cover had reached No. 1 on the Hot 100 since 2001.

They still break through now and then. Before 2023, the last cover to reach the Hot 100 top 10 outside holiday music was Anna Kendrick's "Cups" in 2013, which peaked at No. 6. Then Luke Combs' version of Tracy Chapman's "Fast Car" reached No. 2 in 2023, higher than her original's No. 6 in 1988.

Why did covers fade?

Nobody has pinned it on one cause. These are the money and licensing facts that shape the decision, and which ones matter is still argued about.

  • The publishing goes to the original writerA cover pays the song's writers and publishers in full. The artist covering it earns only on the master. Artists who write their own songs keep both, and hits now routinely credit many writers.
  • Sampling and interpolation took some of the spaceBorrowing a piece of a known song inside a new one has become common. Unlike a straight cover, it needs the publisher's permission, and a sample also needs the master owner's.
  • Video needs a separate licenseThe US compulsory license covers audio only. Putting a cover in a video, an ad, or a film needs a sync license, and sync isn't compulsory. The publisher can say no or name any price.

Was it sync licensing?

Not on its own. The rule that sync isn't compulsory is decades old, so it doesn't explain a recent drop. What changed is where covers get heard: more of them live on YouTube and social video, where the sync question applies. In practice, YouTube's Content ID usually lets a cover video stay up and sends its ad money to the song's owners, not to the person who uploaded it.

Was it radio?

Not as a change. US AM/FM radio has never paid the master, for covers or originals. It pays the songwriters through the PROs. So a cover played on US radio pays the original writer and nothing to the artist who recorded it, but that was just as true in the 1960s.

What does a cover actually get you?

WhoWhat they get from your cover
You, the artistOwnership of the new master and its share of streams and sales, plus SoundExchange money from internet and satellite radio
The original songwriters and publishersAll of the publishing: mechanicals, performance royalties, and their share of any sync
The original master ownerNothing. You didn't use their recording.

The writer's side can be big. Billboard reported that Combs' 2023 "Fast Car" earned Chapman at least $500,000 in publishing in its first few months.

You own the recording. They own the song. Both of you get paid.

Can covers help independent artists support each other?

The money works that way. If you cover another independent artist's released song, every stream, sale, and radio play of your version pays them as the writer. You don't need their permission for an audio release, though asking is a good way to start the relationship, and you both have a reason to share it.

Stuart's take

Covers let you share art you love and still own a master. Independent artists covering each other, an acoustic version here, a post there, is one of the easiest ways to hold each other up.

How do you release a cover legally in the US?

  • 1. Make sure the song has been releasedThe compulsory license only applies once the song has been distributed to the public in the US with the owner's permission. An unreleased song needs the publisher's direct approval.
  • 2. Get the mechanical licenseFor US streaming, services pay mechanicals through The MLC's blanket license. Downloads and physical copies need a mechanical license, which distributors such as DistroKid and CD Baby can arrange, or you can get one through HFA's Songfile.
  • 3. Keep the song the songSection 115 allows changes to fit your style, but not to the basic melody or fundamental character. Rewritten lyrics or a new section need the publisher's permission.
  • 4. Credit the writers correctlyList the original songwriters on your release so the publishing reaches them.
  • 5. Use your own artworkThe original cover art belongs to someone else.
  • 6. Treat video separatelyA cover video technically needs a sync license from the publisher. Most artists rely on Content ID, which usually means the video stays up but the song's owners collect the ad money.

General information, not legal advice. Medleys, translations, and rewritten lyrics usually need direct permission.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need permission to release a cover song?

Not for an audio release in the US, once the song has been released to the public. The compulsory mechanical license under Section 115 lets anyone record it as long as they pay the royalty and do not change the basic melody or character.

Who gets paid when I release a cover?

You own the new master and earn its share. The original songwriters and publishers get all of the publishing: mechanicals, performance royalties, and their share of any sync. The original master owner gets nothing.

Do I need a license to post a cover on YouTube?

Technically, a sync license from the publisher, which is not compulsory. In practice, Content ID usually keeps the video up and sends the ad money to the song's owners.

When did a cover last hit No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100?

According to Chris Dalla Riva's 2023 analysis, no cover had reached No. 1 since 2001. Luke Combs' cover of "Fast Car" reached No. 2 in 2023.

Does a cover played on US radio pay the artist?

Not on AM/FM, which pays only the songwriters and publishers through the PROs. Satellite and internet radio pay the recording through SoundExchange too.
