How to collect every penny from your music

Music businessNo. 03US-basedBy Stuart SingletonUpdated November 17, 20269 min read

Stuart asks

I get asked this all the time, so can you lay it out to make it easy? How does an independent artist collect every penny their music earns, and where does each song need to be registered?

The short answer

Every song earns from two copyrights, and each one gets paid through different doors. For independent artists in the US, collecting all of it comes down to a split sheet, a distributor, a performing rights organization (PRO), SoundExchange, and a publishing administrator to handle the composition side: The MLC, the Harry Fox Agency (HFA), Music Reports (MRI), YouTube, and societies outside the US. Add a US copyright registration so you can enforce it. Anything that isn't registered still gets paid out, just not to you.

This is written for independent artists in the US: you own your songs and recordings, and you work with a distributor rather than a label. If you're signed, your label or publisher handles much of this, and your contract decides who does what. What "independent" really means is its own conversation.

What should you do before registering anything?

Get a split sheet done. It lists every writer, their percentage of the song, their publisher if they have one, their PRO, and their IPI number, the ID their PRO gives each writer. Every registration below asks for the same facts. If they don't match from one place to the next, the money gets stuck.

The usual advice is to sign it before anyone leaves the room. In practice, a song is rarely done when the session ends. People keep writing, a verse gets rewritten, another writer gets brought in weeks later, and a song can change a hundred times between the first demo and the final recording. And if you're the person pushing paperwork on writers who've been at it all day and are trying to get home or to a gig, you may stop getting the call.

So aim for the point when everyone involved agrees the song is done. Mixing is the cherry on top. Mastering is the sprinkles that tell you the sundae is about to go out. Splits need to be settled before mastering, because that's when it's leaving the kitchen. It gets easier the more you do it and the better you know the people you write with.

What's the difference between an ISRC and an ISWC?

They're the two codes that match money to the two copyrights in a song.

CodeWhere it comes from
ISRC identifies one recording (the master)Usually your distributor
ISWC identifies the song (the composition)Your PRO, when you register the song
UPC identifies the release (single, EP, album)Usually your distributor

Distributors handle the recording side. They don't register your song with a PRO, so the ISWC comes from you, your publisher, or your admin company. Put the ISRC on your song registrations wherever there's a field for it. That's what lets the two sides find each other.

Can you buy your own ISRCs?

Yes. Your distributor's free codes work fine, but you can also get your own registrant code from USISRC, the US agency run by the RIAA. It's a one-time $95 fee, and it lets you assign up to 100,000 ISRCs. Then you enter your own code when you upload to any distributor instead of taking theirs. Your recordings carry your registrant prefix rather than a distributor's, and the codes stay the same wherever you release. Anyone else selling ISRCs one at a time is a reseller.

Where does each song need to be registered?

Here's every place, split by who usually does it. Most independent songwriters who are serious about collecting use a publishing administrator for the composition side, because the list of places to register is long and most of them are hard to reach as an individual.

1. Split sheet
Composition		Nothing directly. Every registration below copies from it.
2. Your distributor
Master		Your share of streams and downloads. Gives you the ISRC and UPC, or takes the ISRCs you bought.
3. A PRO: ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, or GMR
Composition		Join as a writer. Your writer's share of performance royalties from radio, TV, venues, and streaming is paid to you directly.
4. SoundExchange
Master		Internet and satellite radio like Pandora and SiriusXM. Register as the featured artist and, if you own the master, as the rights owner.
5. US Copyright Office
Both, registered separately		Pays nothing directly, but you need it to sue for infringement in the US.
Your admin company does theseWhat it collects
6. Your PRO, publisher side
Composition		Registers each song and collects the publisher's share of performance royalties.
7. The MLC
Composition		US mechanical royalties from streaming services under the blanket license. Your PRO does not collect these.
8. HFA and MRI
Composition		Mechanicals that fall outside The MLC: physical copies made by others (13.1¢ per song per copy in 2026), and direct licenses with some services.
9. Societies outside the US
Composition		Performance and mechanical royalties earned in other countries, registered directly instead of waiting on reciprocal deals between PROs.
10. YouTube, publishing side
Composition		The song's share of ad money from videos that use it. The recording side usually goes through your distributor.
11. Lyrics services
Composition		Licensed lyrics on streaming apps and search, through services such as Musixmatch and LyricFind. Some admins and distributors submit these.

Admin companies differ in what they cover, so check the list against your contract. Outside the US, recordings also earn "neighboring rights" through local societies such as PPL in the UK, collected by a neighboring rights service or directly.

An admin takes a commission, typically somewhere in the 10 to 20% range, and you keep ownership of your songs. You can do parts of the composition side yourself. Self-administered songwriters can join The MLC for free and register as their own publisher at a PRO. What's hard to reach alone is HFA, MRI, and the societies in other countries, and keeping every registration current as you release more. Stuart uses an admin company for his own songs.

How much can mechanical royalties be worth?

More than most artists expect. The MLC alone has paid out more than $4.2 billion to songwriters and publishers since it started in 2021, and that's only US streaming and download mechanicals. Mechanicals are the royalty for copying the song onto a stream, a download, or a record, and nobody collects them for you by default. Your distributor pays the recording side. Your PRO pays performance royalties. Mechanicals come through a third door.

The MLC matches what services report to registered songs. In one 2026 payout, it matched a little over 83%. The rest waits for someone to claim it.

It won't wait forever. Starting in April 2027, The MLC plans to begin paying out remaining unclaimed royalties to rightsholders based on market share, so most of that money goes to the biggest catalogs.

If your song isn't registered, your money still gets paid. Just not to you.

In what order should you do it when you release a song?

  • 1. Split sheetOnce everyone agrees the song is done, and before it's mastered.
  • 2. DistributorUpload the release with your ISRCs, or get theirs, plus a UPC, ideally a few weeks before release day.
  • 3. Admin company, or your PRO and The MLCAdd the song with every writer, split, and the ISRC. If you have an admin, they register it with your PRO, The MLC, HFA, MRI, and foreign societies.
  • 4. SoundExchangeOnce, as an artist and as a rights owner. New releases follow from there.
  • 5. US Copyright OfficeWithin three months of release, to keep statutory damages and legal fees available if someone copies it. Current fees are $45 for a single application and $65 for a standard one. An increase was proposed in 2026, so check copyright.gov before you file.

How do you check whether money is already waiting for you?

Search your name and song titles in The MLC's public search, your PRO's repertory search, and SoundExchange's unclaimed search. If a song you wrote shows up with missing writers or no ISWC, or doesn't show up at all, that's money that isn't finding you.

Registering makes sure the money can find you. The next question is how much there actually is, and where it goes before it reaches you.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I need to register a song?

For independent artists in the US: a split sheet, your distributor, a PRO as a writer, SoundExchange, and the US Copyright Office. On the composition side, the song also needs registering with your PRO as publisher, The MLC, HFA and Music Reports, societies outside the US, YouTube, and lyrics services, which a publishing administrator usually handles.

Can I buy my own ISRC codes?

Yes. USISRC, run by the RIAA, sells a registrant code for a one-time $95 that lets you assign up to 100,000 ISRCs, which you can use at any distributor.

What does a publishing administrator do?

It registers your songs with your PRO as publisher, The MLC, HFA, Music Reports, foreign societies, and other pay sources, and collects for a commission while you keep ownership.

Does my distributor register my song with a PRO?

No. Distributors handle the recording side and usually give you an ISRC. The ISWC comes from your PRO when you register the song.

What is the difference between an ISRC and an ISWC?

An ISRC identifies a recording and usually comes from your distributor. An ISWC identifies the song itself and is assigned by your PRO when you register it.

Does my PRO collect mechanical royalties?

Not in the US. US streaming mechanicals under the blanket license are paid to The MLC, which is free for self-administered songwriters. Physical, direct-licensed, and international mechanicals usually come through a publisher or admin company via HFA, Music Reports, and foreign societies.

What happens to royalties for songs that are not registered?

They sit unmatched. The MLC plans to begin paying out remaining unclaimed royalties to rightsholders by market share starting in April 2027, so most of that money goes to the biggest catalogs.

When should I register my copyright?

Within three months of release keeps statutory damages and legal fees available if someone infringes. Current fees are $45 for a single application and $65 for a standard one, with an increase proposed in 2026.
